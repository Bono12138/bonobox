# Stakeholder map for a live decision

Use this map to understand one result that the user is trying to change. It is not a general directory of people and it is not a personality profile.

## Start from the result

Write the desired result and the smallest next transition that is blocked. Include only people who can materially affect that transition through action, information, resources, approval, influence, maintenance, delay, veto or non-obstruction.

Formal authority and practical control may belong to different people. A coordinator, assistant, veteran operator, trusted peer, client, auditor or socially central colleague may matter more than the person highest on the chart.

## Keep provenance visible

For every material claim about a person, mark one of:

- **observed:** the user or an approved source directly saw the behaviour;
- **reported:** another person described it;
- **confirmed judgment:** the user explicitly accepts this interpretation;
- **inference:** available behaviour supports it, but it is not confirmed;
- **hypothesis:** a possible explanation that must be tested;
- **unknown:** missing information that could change the route.

Never turn an inference about motive into a fact. “She delayed the file three times” is an observation. “She wants the project to fail” is a hypothesis unless separately supported.

## Minimum map

For each material actor, record only what affects this decision:

| Question | What to capture |
|---|---|
| Actual role | What must this person do, provide, approve, tolerate or stop doing? |
| Effective power | Can they approve, execute, inform, delay, veto, legitimise, redirect resources or influence the outcome owner? |
| Current payoff | What convenience, credit, control, safety, status or avoided work may the present arrangement provide? |
| Change cost | What extra work, risk, exposure, loss of control or responsibility would the desired result impose? |
| Information | What do they know, misunderstand, conceal or depend on others to explain? |
| Dependencies | Who needs them, and whose support or reaction do they care about? |
| Observed behaviour | What happened in the latest comparable case? |
| Confidence | Which entries are observed, reported, inferred, hypothesised or unknown? |
| Disproof signal | What response would show that this model is wrong? |

## Read the map as a system

Look for:

- a formal supporter who bears the implementation cost and may quietly slow it down;
- a formal approver who follows the judgment of a less visible adviser;
- a beneficiary who has no formal authority but can supply evidence, legitimacy or pressure;
- a person who can kill the result merely by not responding;
- costs being transferred to a person who was never included in the plan;
- public agreement hiding private loss;
- an alliance that exists because interests overlap, not because people like one another;
- a sequence problem where approaching one actor first changes how everyone else responds.

## Do not stop at the map

The map must lead to an action decision:

1. Name the behaviour that must change.
2. Name the actor or small coalition capable of changing it.
3. Explain why the current position persists.
4. Choose what can be changed: information, risk, reward, burden, status, ownership, control, audience, timing, default or sequence.
5. Predict the most likely response and counter-move.
6. Choose a reversible first step and an observation that will update the map.

Then write:

> Today the user bears ___ when ___ does nothing. After the move, the user can ___, while anyone stopping it must ___ and bear or explain ___.

Identify which downstream users, sponsors or adjacent owners gain from the proposed progress and can become a working coalition around a concrete artefact or decision.

If the output ends with a matrix, the analysis is unfinished.
