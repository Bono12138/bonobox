# Active reality inquiry

Use this guide when the Agent must decide what to inspect or ask next.

## 1. Find the information that has decision value

Do not collect background merely because it is available. A gap deserves
attention when materially different answers change at least one of:

- whether an option is possible;
- who can act or block;
- the risk or unacceptable downside;
- the first executable step;
- whether the task should continue, stop, narrow or escalate.

Write the branches before collecting. If all plausible answers lead to the same
action, mark the gap low value and proceed without asking.

## 2. Use the strongest low-friction evidence first

Prefer this order, while respecting project permissions:

1. evidence already present in the task or conversation;
2. actual artefacts used in the last real case;
3. approved system records, queries, logs and knowledge sources;
4. direct observation of current work;
5. a small reversible probe that exposes the real constraint;
6. a targeted question to the person who directly knows or decides;
7. an explicit assumption with branches when evidence cannot be obtained.

The order is not absolute. A current system record may outrank an old report;
the actual operator may be more reliable than an obsolete procedure. Record why
the selected source can answer this gap.

## 3. Choose the next inquiry

Rank open gaps by:

- decision impact;
- chance that the answer will separate the available actions;
- reliability and freshness of the available source;
- time and effort needed;
- permission and reversibility;
- urgency of the decision.

Investigate the highest-value accessible gap first. Batch actions only when the
same source can answer them without creating extra work.

## 4. Ask a branch-changing question

Avoid broad prompts such as “please explain the process” or “provide the data
dictionary”. Ask for the smallest observable fact that separates actions.

Examples:

- “上一次这个结果真正发出去前，最后是谁改了文件？”
- “你现在能直接打开这张表，还是每次都要找数据团队导出？”
- “如果本周不处理，谁会在什么节点来追这件事？”
- “把最近一次实际使用的版本给我就够了，不需要重新整理流程。”

Do not present an interrogation. Explain why the answer matters when that helps
the user respond. Ask one to three questions, use the answers, then decide
whether another round is still necessary.

Assume the user may not volunteer the missing context and may not know formal
labels or exact measurements. Keep ownership of the inquiry. Turn abstract
conditions into ordinary observations:

- ask whether everyone can hear one another or the workplace is split into
  separate areas, rather than asking for floor area;
- ask who normally dares to joke with or correct the person, rather than asking
  for a stakeholder power map;
- ask what the person visibly did immediately before the problem appeared,
  rather than asking for a causal assessment;
- ask whether the outcome disappeared, reduced, stayed the same or created a
  new problem, rather than asking whether the intervention was successful.

If the user cannot answer, change the source, use a visible proxy, or design a
small reversible observation. Do not repeat the same abstract question.

### Reconstruct a confused multi-party scene with low burden

When somebody mixes the goal, proposed method, participants and sequence, do
not send a long list of sensitive or technical questions. First extract the
facts already stated, then privately sketch two or three plausible structures.
Each sketch should show:

- who is involved and whether each person or organisation is on one side,
  another side or only an intermediary;
- where each participant and relevant asset is located;
- which way the object, resource, data, service or benefit moves;
- who is responsible for, authorises or promises what;
- which commitments and actions happen first;
- what exists now, what is only planned, and what final result is wanted.

Keep invented links visibly hypothetical. Present the few sketches in plain
language and ask which is closest, followed by at most one decisive correction.
This is often more answerable than asking six separate questions. Never ask the
other person to reorganise the whole story or “send the correct version”.

## 5. Search for alternate paths through the situation

When the obvious action is socially, politically or practically blocked,
identify the exact state transition that cannot occur. Then search beyond the
formal actor list for a carrier, setting or mechanism that can make that
transition happen.

Possible carriers include trusted peers, informal influencers, naturally
outspoken people, existing routines, public-but-ordinary conversations,
physical layout, timing, default rules and reversible probes. An overheard
conversation can sometimes transmit both a diagnostic signal and a feasible
remedy without forcing the user into direct confrontation.

Before using an indirect scene, establish the audience size, source certainty,
relationships and status, local tolerance for jokes, likelihood that the user
will be exposed, and how the interaction can stop if it lands badly. Creative
does not mean theatrical at any cost; the route must reduce the real obstacle.

## 6. Learn from missing and conflicting evidence

Missing evidence is itself informative only within limits. It may indicate that
the process is informal, ownership is unclear or the requested object never
existed; it may also mean the Agent searched the wrong place or lacks access.
Keep these explanations separate.

When sources conflict:

- compare time, scope and directness;
- distinguish written rule from observed practice;
- identify who produced each source and for what purpose;
- seek the smallest additional observation that separates the explanations;
- preserve the conflict if it cannot be resolved before the decision.

## 7. Continue through observed feedback

After an action or probe, ask what actually happened. Update the working
hypothesis before giving the next step. A performed action is not a solved
problem, and a failed result does not prove that the user executed badly.

Stop only when the user confirms an acceptable outcome, chooses to stop, the
remaining uncertainty cannot change the current action, or progress needs
unavailable authority or evidence.

## 8. Stop deliberately

Stop collecting when one of these is true:

- the material branch has been resolved with adequate evidence;
- the remaining gap cannot change the current action;
- the decision must proceed under a named assumption;
- the cost or delay of more collection exceeds its decision value;
- required access or authority is unavailable;
- a human choice, not more evidence, is now required.

Do not use “more research” to postpone a decision. Do not use urgency to hide a
critical unknown that could make the action unsafe or impossible.
